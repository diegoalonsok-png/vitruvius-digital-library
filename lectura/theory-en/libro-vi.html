Traducción

Aristippus, the Socratic philosopher, shipwrecked and cast ashore on the coast of Rhodes, noticed geometrical figures drawn in the sand and is said to have cried out to his companions: “Let us be of good hope, for I see the traces of men.” He made at once for the city of Rhodes and went straight to the gymnasium, where, discoursing on philosophy, he was rewarded with gifts so generous that he could provide not only for himself but also for those who were with him, with clothing and everything else needed for life. When his companions wished to return home and asked what message he would have them carry back, he bade them say this: that children ought to be furnished with such possessions and such provisions for the journey as could swim ashore with them even from a shipwreck.

Traducción

For these are the true safeguards of life: neither the adverse storms of fortune, nor upheavals in public affairs, nor the devastation of war can harm them. Theophrastus develops the same idea, urging people to become learned rather than to put their trust in wealth. The educated person alone is not a stranger in foreign lands or left without friends when family and companions are lost; in every city such a person is a citizen and can face the hazards of fortune without fear. By contrast, one who trusts in good luck rather than learning walks on slippery paths and struggles through a fragile, uncertain life.

Traducción

Epicurus likewise says that fortune grants the wise relatively little, whereas the greatest and most necessary matters are governed by thought and judgment. Many other philosophers held the same view. The authors of ancient Greek comedy — Crates, Chionides, Aristophanes and, above all, Alexis — expressed similar ideas on stage. Alexis praises the Athenians because, whereas Greek laws generally require children to support their parents, the Athenians impose that duty only in respect of parents who have taught their children an art. Fortune may take away everything it has given; learning, once joined to the mind, remains steadfast to the end of life.

Traducción

I, however, Caesar, have not applied myself to making money by my art; I have preferred slender means with a good name to abundance with disrepute. For this reason I have gained little renown. Yet, now that these volumes are published, I hope to be known even to posterity. Nor should it surprise anyone that I am unknown to so many. Other architects solicit and canvass for commissions; but I was taught by my masters that one should undertake a charge when asked, not by asking, because a freeborn man’s complexion is changed by shame when he petitions for something that arouses suspicion. For it is those who confer a favor who are courted, not those who receive it. What, then, are we to suppose a man suspects when he is asked to entrust the spending of his patrimony to the goodwill of a petitioner, if not that the petitioner judges the work worth doing for his own plunder and profit?

Craftsmen themselves trained only their sons or relatives, forming them as upright men to whose integrity funds for important works could safely be entrusted. Yet when I see so great a discipline handled at the whim of the uneducated and inexperienced, including men who know neither architecture nor even the craft of building, I cannot but commend those householders who, trusting in their literary education, build for themselves. If the work must be entrusted to the inexperienced, they judge themselves more entitled to spend their money according to their own judgment than according to someone else’s.

Accordingly, since in the fifth book I have written of the fitness of public works, in this volume I shall set out the principles of private buildings and the commensurability of their proportions.

Latín

Haec autem ita erunt recte disposita, si primo animadversum fuerit quibus regionibus aut quibus inclinationibus mundi constituantur. namque aliter Aegypto, aliter Hispania, non eodem modo Ponto, dissimiliter Romae, item ceteris terrarum et regionum proprietatibus oportere videntur constitui genera aedificiorum, quod alia parte solis cursu premitur tellus, alia longe ab eo distat, alia per medium temperatur. igitur uti constitutio mundi ad terrae spatium inclinatione signiferi circuli et solis cursu disparibus qualitatibus naturaliter est conlocata, ad eundem modum etiam ad regionum rationes caelique varietates videntur aedificiorum debere dirigi conlocationes.

Traducción

These buildings will be rightly disposed if attention is first paid to the regions and latitudes of the world in which they are set. For it seems that the kinds of building ought to be constituted in one way in Egypt, in another in Spain, not in the same way in Pontus, differently at Rome, and so on according to the properties of other lands and regions; for in one part the earth is oppressed by the course of the sun, in another it lies far from it, and in another it is tempered by an intermediate position. Therefore, just as the constitution of the heavens in relation to the extent of the earth is naturally disposed with unequal qualities by the inclination of the zodiac and the course of the sun, so the disposition of buildings ought likewise to be governed by the character of each region and the variety of its climate.

Thus what nature harms by excess must be corrected by art. In the other regions, too, buildings must be tempered in the same way, according to how the climate is disposed in relation to the inclination of the world.
