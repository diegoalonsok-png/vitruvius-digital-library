Vitr., De arch. VIII.praef.1

Latín

De septem sapientibus Thales Milesius omnium rerum principium aquam est professus, Heraclitus ignem, Magorum sacerdotes aquam et ignem, Euripides, auditor Anaxagorae, quem philosophum Athenienses scaenicum appellaverunt, aera et terram, eamque e caelestium imbrium conceptionibus inseminatam fetus gentium et omnium animalium in mundo procreavisse, et quae ex ea essent prognata, cum dissolverentur temporum necessitate coacta in eandem redire, quaeque de aere nascerentur, item in caeli regiones reverti neque interitiones recipere et dissolutione mutata in eam recidere, in qua ante fuerant, proprietatem. Pythagoras vero, Empedocles, Epicharmos aliique physici et philosophi haec principia esse quattuor proposuerunt: aerem, ignem, terram, aquam, eorumque inter se cohaerentiam naturali figuratione e generum discriminibus efficere qualitates.

Traducción

Among the Seven Sages, Thales of Miletus held that water was the first principle of all things; Heraclitus favored fire, and the priests of the Magi both water and fire. Euripides, a pupil of Anaxagoras whom the Athenians called “the philosopher of the stage”, proposed air and earth: the latter, fertilised by rain from the heavens, brought forth human beings and other living creatures. What arose from earth would return to it when dissolved by time, while what arose from air would return to the regions of the sky, not perishing but reverting through dissolution to its former nature. Pythagoras, Empedocles, Epicharmus and other natural philosophers proposed four principles — air, fire, earth and water — whose natural combination produces different qualities.

Latín

Traducción

We observe that the elements not only produce living things: without their power nothing can be nourished, grow or be sustained. Bodies cannot live without a sufficient supply of breath, as incoming air maintains the continual expansion and contraction of breathing. Unless the body has the proper measure of heat, it lacks vitality and strength, and food cannot be digested at the necessary temperature. Unless its members are nourished by food from the earth, they fail, deprived of the contribution of the earthly element.

Vitr., De arch. VIII.praef.3

Latín

Animalia vero si fuerint sine umoris potestate, exsanguinata et exsucata [a principiorum liquore] interarescent. Igitur divina mens, quae proprie necessaria essent gentibus, non constituit difficilia et cara, uti sunt margaritae, aurum, argentum ceteraque, quae neque corpus nec natura desiderat, sed sine quibus mortalium vita non potest esse tuta, effudit ad manum parata per omnem mundum. Itaque ex his, si quid forte defit in corpore spiritus, ad restituendum aer adsignatus id praestat. Apparatus autem ad auxilia caloris solis impetus et ignis inventus tutiorem efficit vitam. Item terrenus fructus escarum praestans copiis supervacuis desiderationibus alit et nutrit animales pascendo continenter. Aqua vero non solum potus sed infinitas usu praebendo necessitates, gratas, quod est gratuita, praestat utilitates.

Traducción

Animals deprived of moisture would likewise lose their blood and bodily fluids and dry up. The divine mind therefore did not make the necessities of human life difficult and costly to obtain, as are pearls, gold, silver and other things that neither the body nor nature requires. Instead, it made readily available throughout the world the things without which mortal life cannot be sustained. If the body lacks breath, air restores it. The warmth of the Sun and the use of fire provide the heat that makes life more secure. The produce of the earth supplies abundant food, continually feeding and sustaining living creatures. Water provides not only drink but countless other necessities, and its usefulness is all the more welcome because it is freely available.

Latín

Est enim maxime necessaria et ad vitam et ad delectationes, et ad usum cotidianum Ea autem erit facilior, si erunt fontes aperti et fluentes. Sin autem non profluent, quaerenda sub terra sunt capita et colligenda. Traducción

For water is most necessary both for life and for its pleasures, and for daily use. It will be the easier to obtain if there are open and flowing springs. But where none flow forth, the sources must be sought and gathered underground. The search is to be made thus: let a man lie face downward, before the sun has risen, in the places in which it is to be sought, and with the chin placed and supported on the ground, those regions are to be looked for; for in this way the sight will not err higher than it should be, when the chin is motionless, but will designate a balanced height in the regions with a certain boundary. Then, in those places where moistures are seen to be bubbling and rising into the air, there is to be dug; for this sign cannot be made in a dry place.

Vitr., De arch. VIII.1.2

Latín

Item animadvertendum est quaerentibus aquam, quo genere sint loca; certa enim sunt, in quibus nascitur. In creta tenuis et exilis et non alta est copia; ea erit non optimo sapore. Item sabulone soluto tenuis, sed inferioris loci invenietur; ea erit limosa et insuavis. Terra autem nigra sudores et stillae exiles inveniuntur, quae ex hibernis tempestatibus collectae in spissis et solidis locis subsidunt; haec habent optimum saporem. Glarea vero mediocres et non certae venae reperiuntur; hae quoque sunt egregia suavitate. Item sabulone masculo harenaque carbunculo certiores [et stabiliores] sunt copiae; eaeque sunt bono sapore. Rubro saxo et bonae et copiosae, si non per intervenia dilabantur et liquescant. Sub radicibus autem montium et in saxis silicibus uberiores et affluentiores; eaeque frigidiores sunt et salubriores. Campestribus autem fontibus salsae, graves, tepidae, non suaves, nisi quae ex montibus sub terra submanantes erumpunt in medios campos ibique arborum umbris contectae praestant montanorum fontium suavitatem.

Traducción

Those searching for water should also examine the nature of the ground, since different kinds occur in particular formations. In clay the supply is slight, shallow and poor in taste. In loose sand it is likewise scanty, though found at a greater depth, and it is muddy and unpleasant. In black earth, small seepages and droplets collect from winter rain and settle in dense, compact ground; these have an excellent taste. Gravel contains moderate and uncertain veins, also of excellent sweetness. In coarse sand and carbunculus, supplies are more reliable [and more constant], and their taste is good. In red rock the supply is abundant and good, provided that it does not escape and disperse through intervening fissures. Beneath mountain slopes and in siliceous rock it is more plentiful, colder and more wholesome. Springs in the plains, by contrast, are saline, heavy, lukewarm and unpleasant, except where water runs underground from the mountains and emerges in the middle of a plain: shaded there by trees, it retains the sweetness of mountain springs.

Latín

Traducción

The signs, which are written above, will be found growing in the following types of land: the slender rush, the wandering willow, the alder, the vitex, the reed, the ivy, and other such plants, which cannot grow by themselves without moisture. But they are usually grown in lakes, which, when they sit in them, receive more moisture from the rains and fields than from the rest of the land, and because of their capacity, they retain moisture longer during the winter. We must not believe these, but we must seek in which regions and lands, not lakes, these signs grow, not sown, but naturally produced by themselves.

Vitr., De arch. VIII.1.4

Latín

In quibus si eae significabuntur inventiones, sic erunt experiundae. Fodiatur quoquoversus locus latus ne minus pedes <tres, altus pedes> quinque, in eoque conlocetur circiter solis occasum scaphium aereum aut plumbeum aut pelvis. Ex his quod erit paratum, id intrinsecus oleo ungatur ponaturque inversum, et summa fossura operiatur harundinibus aut fronde, supra terra obruatur; tum postero die aperiatur, et si in vaso stillae sudoresque erunt, is locus habebit aquam.

Traducción

Where these signs indicate water, test the ground as follows. Dig a pit at least three feet across in each direction and five feet deep. At about sunset place in it a bronze or lead vessel, or a basin, whichever is available, first coating its inner surface with oil and setting it upside down. Cover the opening of the pit with reeds or leaves and lay earth over them. Open it the following day: if droplets of condensation appear in the vessel, the place contains water.

Traducción

A fleece of wool from which water can be squeezed the following day likewise indicates an abundant supply. A lamp, prepared with oil and lit before being enclosed there, will indicate water if it has not burnt out by the next day, still contains oil and wick, and is found damp: warmth attracts moisture. Similarly, if a fire lit there heats the earth and causes a misty vapor to rise, water is present.

Vitr., De arch. VIII.1.7

Latín

Intervallaque montium maxime recipiunt imbres et propter silvarum crebritatem nives ab umbris arborum et montium ibi diutius conservantur, deinde liquatae per terrae venas percolantur et ita perveniunt ad infimas montium radices, ex quibus profluentes fontium erumpunt ructus. Campestribus autem locis contrario non possunt habere copias. Nam quaecumque sunt, non possunt habere salubritatem, quod solis vehemens impetus propter nullam obstantiam umbrarum eripit exhauriendo fervens ex planitie camporum umorem, et si quae sunt aquae apparentes, ex his, quod est levissimum tenuissimumque et subtili salubritate, aer avocans dissipat in impetum caeli, quaeque gravissimae duraeque et insuaves sunt partes, eae in fontibus campestribus relinquuntur.

Traducción

The valleys between mountains receive the most rain. Dense woodland and the shade of trees and mountains preserve the snow there for longer; when it melts, it filters through veins in the earth and reaches the lowest mountain slopes, where it gushes out in springs. In the plains, by contrast, supplies cannot be abundant or wholesome. With no shade to obstruct it, the Sun’s fierce heat draws moisture from the exposed ground. Of any water that remains visible, the air carries away and disperses the lightest, finest and most wholesome parts, leaving the heaviest, hardest and least pleasant components in the springs of the plains.

Vitr., De arch. VIII.2.1

Latín

Itaque, quae ex imbribus aqua colligitur, salubriores habet virtutes, quod eligitur ex omnibus fontibus levissimis subtilibusque tenuitatibus, deinde per aeris exercitationem percolata tempestatibus liquescendo pervenit ad terram. Etiamque non crebriter in campis confluunt imbres, sed in montibus aut ad ipsos montes, ideo quod umores ex terra matutino solis ortu moti cum sunt egressi, in quamcumque partem caeli sunt proclinati, trudunt aera; deinde, cum sunt moti, propter vacuitatem loci post se recipiunt aeris ruentis undas.

Traducción

Therefore, the water that is collected from the rains has more salubrious virtues, because it is selected from all the lightest and most subtle springs, and then, filtered by the exercise of the air, it reaches the ground, liquefied by storms. Moreover, the rains do not often flow in the plains, but in the mountains or on the mountains themselves, for this reason that the moistures, when they have emerged from the earth at the morning sunrise, are moved by the air in whatever part of the sky they are inclined to; then, when they are moved, because of the emptiness of the place, they receive the waves of the rushing air behind them.

Traducción

The vaults over the hot rooms of baths cannot have springs above them. Instead, the air inside, heated by the fire at the furnaces, draws water from the floors, carries it up into the curved vaults and holds it there, because hot vapor always rises. At first, while the quantity is small, it does not release the moisture. Once more has accumulated, however, it can no longer support the weight, and water drips onto the bathers’ heads. In the same way, atmospheric air warmed by the Sun draws moisture from every place and gathers it into clouds. Heat causes the earth to give off moisture just as it causes the human body to sweat.

Traducción

Once these arrangements have been made and water has been reached, line the well with open-jointed masonry so that the water-bearing veins are not blocked.

Vitr., De arch. VIII.6.14

Latín

Sin autem loca dura erunt aut nimium venae penitus fuerint, tunc signinis operibus ex tectis aut superioribus locis excipiendae sunt copiae. In signinis autem operibus haec sunt facienda. Uti harena primum purissima asperrimaque paretur, caementum de silice frangatur ne gravius quam librarium, calx quam vehementissima mortario mixta, ita ut quinque partes harenae ad duas respondeant. Eorum fossa ad libramentum altitudinis, quod est futurum, calcetur vectibus ligneis ferratis.

Traducción

If the ground is hard or the water-bearing veins lie too deep, collect supplies from roofs or higher ground in cisterns built in opus signinum. Prepare very clean, coarse sand, and break flint into pieces weighing no more than a pound each. Mix very strong lime in the mixing vessel, using five parts sand to two of lime. Ram the material into the trenches to the intended wall height with iron-shod wooden rammers.

Vitr., De arch. VIII.6.15

Latín

Parietibus calcatis, in medio quod erit terrenum, exinaniatur ad libramentum infimum parietum. Hoc exaequato solum calcetur ad crassitudinem, quae constituta fuerit. Ea autem si duplicia aut triplicia facta fuerint, uti percolationibus transmutari possint, multo salubriorem [et suaviorem] aquae usum efficient; limus enim cum habuerit, quo subsidat, limpidior fiet et sine odoribus conservabit saporem. Si non, salem addi necesse erit et extenuari.

Traducción

After the walls have been rammed, remove the earth between them down to their lowest level. Level the bottom and ram the floor to the prescribed thickness. Two or three connected cisterns, allowing water to pass through successive stages of filtration, make the supply much healthier [and sweeter]. When the silt has somewhere to settle, the water becomes clearer and retains its taste without an offensive smell. Otherwise, salt must be added to refine it.
