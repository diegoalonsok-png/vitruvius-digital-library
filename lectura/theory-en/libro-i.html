Latín

Architecti est scientia pluribus disciplinis et variis eruditionibus ornata, cuius iudicio probantur omnia quae ab ceteris artibus perficiuntur opera. Ea nascitur ex fabrica et ratiocinatione. Fabrica est continuata ac trita usus meditatio, quae manibus perficit[ur] e materia cuiuscumque generis opus [est] ad propositum deformationis. Traducción

The architect’s knowledge embraces many disciplines and varied forms of learning; by its judgment, all works accomplished by the other arts are assessed. It arises from fabrica and ratiocinatio. Fabrica is the continuous, practised exercise of making: through manual work and materials of whatever kind, it brings a work into being according to its intended design. Ratiocinatio, by contrast, enables one to demonstrate and explain constructed works according to the proper proportion of skill and reason.

He must possess natural talent and be receptive to instruction, for neither talent without training nor training without talent can produce an accomplished practitioner.

Traducción

The architect must also know medicine, because of the inclinations of the sky, which the Greeks call klimata, the qualities of the air, the healthfulness or unhealthfulness of places and the uses of water. Without this knowledge no healthy dwelling can be built. He must understand the legal provisions needed for buildings: party walls, boundaries, rainwater runoff, drains, rights of light and water supply. Architects must know these matters before construction begins, so that disputes are not left to householders after the work is complete and so that carefully drafted contracts protect both parties. If a contract is expertly drawn up, each can discharge his obligations to the other without deception or prejudice.

Traducción

Those instructed in varied learning from an early age therefore recognize the same characteristics throughout the branches of knowledge and the connections among them, and so learn more easily. Thus Pytheus, one of the ancient architects, who designed the celebrated temple of Minerva at Priene, states in his commentaries that an architect ought to be able to accomplish more in all the arts and disciplines than those who have brought a single field to the highest distinction through their own diligence and practice. This, however, cannot be achieved in reality.

Vitr., De arch. I.1.15

Latín

Igitur in hac re Pytheos errasse videtur quod non animadvertit ex duabus rebus singulas artes esse compositas, ex opere et eius ratiocinatione. Ex his autem unum proprium esse eorum qui singulis rebus sunt exercitati, id est operis effectus, alterum commune cum omnibus doctis, id est rationem, uti medicis et musicis et de venarum rythmo et pedum [motus], at si vulnus mederi aut aegrum eripere de periculo oportuerit, non accedet musicus, sed id opus proprium erit medici. Traducción

Therefore, in this matter Pythius seems to have erred because he did not observe that the individual arts are composed of two things, from work and its reasoning. Of these, however, one is proper to those who are trained in each thing, that is, the effect of the work, the other is common to all learned men, that is, reasoning, as is the case with physicians and musicians concerning the rhythm of the veins and the movement of the foot. But if it is necessary to heal a wound or rescue a patient from danger, the musician will not come, but that will be the proper work of the physician. Likewise, the organ will be played not by the physician but by the musician, so that the ears may receive pleasure from the songs.

Traducción

Similarly, astronomers and musicians share discussion of the correspondence between the stars and musical consonances, in squares and trines, and in the intervals of a fourth and a fifth; they share with geometers the theory of sight, which the Greeks call logos optikos. Many matters, or indeed all, are common to the disciplines at the level of theoretical discussion. The practical execution of works, however, brought to refinement by hand and skilled manipulation, belongs to those trained to practise a particular art. It is therefore amply sufficient for the architect to have a moderate knowledge of the parts and principles of each discipline that are necessary for architecture, so that he is not found wanting when required to judge and approve matters concerning them.

The kinds of arrangement, which in Greek are called ἰδέαι, are these: the ground plan (ichnographia), the elevation (orthographia), and the perspective (scaenographia). Likewise, the perspective is the sketch of the facade together with its receding sides, with all the lines converging upon the center of a circle. These arise from reflection and invention. Reflection is the studious, industrious, and vigilant care devoted to the pleasurable accomplishment of a design. Invention, for its part, is the unravelling of obscure problems and the reasoned discovery of a new principle through a lively agility of mind. Such are the determinations of arrangement.

On a leveled surface let the center be marked with the letter A, and the tip of the gnomon's morning shadow with the letter B; then, opening the compasses from the center A to the shadow-mark B, describe the line of the circumference. Setting the gnomon back in its former place, one must wait until the shadow shortens and then lengthens again, until the afternoon shadow equals the morning shadow and touches the circumference at the point that shall be the letter C. Then from the marks B and C, with the compasses, strike two arcs crossing at the point that shall be D; and through the intersection D and the center let a line be carried to the circumference, upon which line shall be the letters E and F. This line will be the indicator of the southern and northern quarters.

Traducción

Next, a sixteenth part of the whole circumference is to be taken with the compasses. Setting the compass-point on the meridian line where it meets the circle at the southern end, marked with the letter E, make two marks to right and left, where the letters G and H shall be placed; likewise, at the northern end, marked with the letter F, set the compass-point and make marks to right and left, where the letters I and K shall fall; then draw lines through the center, from G to K and from H to I. Thus the space from G to H will be the sector of the South wind (Auster) and the meridian quarter, and the space from I to K that of the North wind (Septentrio). The remaining parts—three on the right and three on the left—are to be divided equally: to the east, where the letters L and M stand; and to the west, where the letters N and O stand. From M to O and from L to N let lines be drawn through the center. Thus there will be eight equal spaces for the winds around the circumference. When the diagram is so drawn, the angles of the octagon, beginning from the south, will be these: the letter G at the angle between Eurus and Auster; H between Auster and Africus; N between Africus and Favonius; O between Favonius and Caurus; K between Caurus and Septentrio; I between Septentrio and Aquilo; L between Aquilo and Solanus; and M between Solanus and Eurus. When these are completed, a chord is set between the angles of the octagon, and so the divisions of the lanes and streets are directed.

Traducción

Once the lanes and streets have been laid out, sites must be chosen for sacred temples, the forum and other public places, with regard to the city’s convenience and common use. In a coastal town the forum should be near the harbour; in an inland town, at its center. Temples of the gods under whose special protection the city stands, such as Jupiter, Juno and Minerva, should occupy the highest place, from which most of the walls can be seen. Mercury’s temple belongs in the forum or trading center, as do those of Isis and Serapis; Apollo’s and Father Liber’s near the theater; Hercules’s near the circus in towns without gymnasia or amphitheatres; Mars’s outside the city beside the exercise ground; and Venus’s likewise near the harbour. Etruscan haruspical writings prescribe that the shrines of Venus, Vulcan and Mars stand outside the walls, so that erotic passion does not become habitual among the young and the matrons, and so that Vulcan’s power, summoned beyond the walls through rites and sacrifices, appears to free buildings from fear of fire. Mars’s divinity, consecrated outside the walls, is to prevent armed discord among the citizens and defend the city from its enemies.
