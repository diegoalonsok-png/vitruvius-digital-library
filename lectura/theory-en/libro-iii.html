Vitr., De arch. III.praef.1

Latín

Delphicus Apollo Socratem omnium sapientissimum Pythiae responsis est professus. Is autem memoratur prudenter doctissimeque dixisse, oportuisse hominum pectora fenestrata et aperta esse, uti non occultos haberent sensus sed patentes ad considerandum. Utinam vero rerum natura sententiam eius secuta explicata et apparentia ea constituisset! Si enim ita fuisset, non solum laudes aut vitia animorum ad manum aspicerentur, sed etiam disciplinarum scientiae sub oculorum consideratione subiectae non incertis iudiciis probarentur, sed et doctis et scientibus auctoritas egregia et stabilis adderetur ipsique artifices pellicerent sua prudentia, ⟨qui, etiam⟩si non pecunia sint copiosi seu vetustate officinarum habuerint notitiam aut etiam gratia forensi et eloquentia, cum fuerint parati, pro industria studiorum auctoritates possent habere, ut eis, quod profitentur scire, id crederetur. Igitur quoniam haec non ita, sed uti natura rerum voluit, sunt constituta, non efficitur, ut possint homines obscuratis sub pectoribus ingeniis scientias artificiorum penitus latentes, quemadmodum sint, iudicare.

Traducción

The Delphic Apollo, through the responses of the Pythia, proclaimed Socrates the wisest of all men. Socrates is remembered as having said, with prudence and great learning, that men’s breasts ought to have been fitted with windows and left open, so that their thoughts would not remain hidden but be exposed to examination. Would that nature had followed his judgment and made them manifest and visible! Then not only would the merits or faults of minds be seen at a glance, but the knowledge of the various disciplines, placed before the eyes for examination, would not be exposed to uncertain judgments. The learned and knowledgeable would receive firm and lasting authority, and craftsmen themselves would attract confidence through their understanding: even if they were not wealthy, known through the long standing of their workshops, or furnished with favor in the forum and eloquence, their preparation would win authority commensurate with their diligent studies, so that people would believe that they knew what they professed to know. Since things are constituted otherwise, as nature willed, and men’s talents remain concealed within their breasts, people cannot judge the knowledge peculiar to each art, which lies deeply hidden.

Latín

Maxime autem id animadvertere possumus ab antiquis statuariis et pictoribus, quod ex his, qui dignitatis notas et commendationis gratiam habuerunt, aeterna memoria ad posteritatem sunt permanentes, uti Myron, Polycletus, Phidias, Lysippus ceterique, qui nobilitatem ex arte sunt consecuti. Namque ut civitatibus magnis aut regibus aut civibus nobilibus opera fecerunt, ita id sunt adepti. At qui non minore studio et ingenio sollertiaque fuerunt nobilibus et humili fortuna civibus non minus egregie perfecta fecerunt opera, nullam memoriam sunt adsecuti, quod hi non ab industria neque artis sollertia sed a felicitate fuerunt deserti, ut Hegias Atheniensis, Chion Corinthius, Myagrus Phocaeus, Pharax Ephesius, Boedas Byzantius etiamque alii plures. Non minus item pictores, uti Aristomenes Thasius, Polycles et Androcydes ⟨Cyzice⟩ni, Theo Magnes ceterique, quos neque industria neque artis studium neque sollertia defecit, sed aut rei familiaris exiguitas aut inbecillitas fortunae seu in ambitione certationis contrariorum superatio obstitit eorum dignitati.

Traducción

This can be seen above all in the sculptors and painters of old. Those who enjoyed marks of distinction and the favor of recommendation remain in everlasting memory among posterity—Myron, Polyclitus, Phidias, Lysippus and the others who won renown through their art. They achieved it because they made works for great cities, kings or noble citizens. Others, with no less zeal, talent and skill, made works no less admirably finished for citizens both noble and of modest means, yet obtained no remembrance: they were forsaken not by diligence or artistic skill, but by good fortune. Such were Hegias of Athens, Chion of Corinth, Myagrus of Phocaea, Pharax of Ephesus, Boedas of Byzantium and many more. Likewise among painters there were Aristomenes of Thasos, Polycles and Androcydes of Cyzicus, Theon of Magnesia and the others, who lacked neither diligence, artistic commitment nor skill, but whose standing was obstructed by a small patrimony, adverse fortune or the superiority of rivals in the competition for distinction.

Vitr., De arch. III.praef.3

Latín

Nec tamen est admirandum, si propter ignotitiam artis virtutes obscurantur, sed maxime indignandum, cum etiam saepe eblandiatur gratia conviviorum a veris iudiciis falsam probationem. Ergo, uti Socrati placuit, si ita sensus et sententiae scientiaeque disciplinis auctae perspicuae et perlucidae fuissent, non gratia neque ambitio valeret, sed si qui veris certisque laboribus doctrinarum pervenissent ad scientiam summam, eis ultro opera traderentur. Quoniam autem ea non sunt inlustria neque apparentia in aspectu, ut putamus oportuisse, et animadverto potius indoctos quam doctos gratia superare, non esse certandum iudicans cum indoctis ambitione, potius his praeceptis editis ostendam nostrae scientiae virtutem.

Traducción

It is no wonder that merit is obscured through ignorance of the art; but it is especially a cause for indignation when favor won at banquets often coaxes a false approval from true judgment. As Socrates wished, had perceptions, opinions and knowledge enriched by the disciplines been clear and transparent, neither favor nor ambition would prevail: works would be entrusted spontaneously to those who had attained the highest knowledge through genuine and sound scholarly labor. Since these things are neither manifest nor visible, as we think they ought to be, and I observe that the unlearned more often surpass the learned through favor, I judge that I should not compete with the unlearned in ambition. I shall instead show the worth of our knowledge by publishing these precepts.

Vitr., De arch. III.praef.4

Itaque, imperator, in primo volumine tibi de arte et quas habeat ea virtutes quibusque disciplinis oporteat esse auctum architectum exposui, et subieci causas quid ita earum oporteat eum esse peritum, rationesque summae architecturae partitione distribui finitionibusque terminavi. deinde quod erat primum et necessarium, de moenibus quemadmodum eligantur loci salubres ratiocinationibus explicui, ventique qui sint et e quibus <regionibus> singuli spirant deformationibus grammicis ostendi, platearumque et vicorum uti emendate fiant distributiones in moenibus docui et ita finitionem primo volumine constitui. item in secundo de materia quas habeat in operibus utilitates et quibus virtutibus e natura rerum est comparata peregi. nunc in tertio de deorum inmortalium aedibus sacris dicam et uti oportet perscriptas exponam.

Traducción

Accordingly, Emperor, in the first book I set forth for you what the art is, what virtues it possesses, and in what disciplines the architect ought to be trained; I added the reasons why he must be skilled in them, distributed the whole of architecture by a division, and defined it by definitions. Then, treating what was first and most necessary, I explained by reasoned argument how, in laying out city walls, to choose healthy sites; I showed by linear diagrams which the winds are and from what regions each blows; I taught how the streets and lanes within the walls may be correctly laid out, and so brought the first book to a close. In the second I dealt with materials—what advantages they afford in works, and with what qualities Nature has endowed them. Now, in the third, I shall speak of the sacred temples of the immortal gods and set them forth, as is fitting, in full detail.

Vitr., De arch. III.1.1

Latín

Aedium compositio constat ex symmetria, cuius rationem diligentissime architecti tenere debent. ea autem paritur a proportione, quae graece ἀναλογία dicitur. proportio est ratae partis membrorum in omni opere totiusque commodulatio, ex qua ratio efficitur symmetriarum. namque non potest aedis ulla sine symmetria atque proportione rationem habere compositionis, nisi uti hominis bene figurati <> membrorum habuerit exactam rationem.

Traducción

The composition of temples depends on symmetria, whose principles architects must observe with the greatest care. Symmetria arises from proportion, which the Greeks call analogia (ἀναλογία). Proportion is the commensuration of the members and the whole of a work with a determined part as their common measure; from it the system of commensurability is established. No temple can have a coherent composition without symmetria and proportion, unless its members stand in an exact relationship comparable to that of a well-formed human body.

Vitr., De arch. III.1.2

Latín

Corpus enim hominis ita natura composuit, uti os capitis a mento ad frontem summam et radices imas capilli esset decimae partis, item manus pansa ab articulo ad extremum medium digitum tantundem, caput a mento ad summum verticem octavae, cum cervicibus imis ab summo pectore ad imas radices capillorum sextae, ⟨a medio pectore⟩ ad summum verticem quartae. Ipsius autem oris altitudinis tertia est pars ab imo mento ad imas nares, nasum ab imis naribus ad finem mediûm superciliorum tantundem, ab ea fine ad imas radices capilli frons efficitur item tertiae partis. Pes vero altitudinis corporis sextae, cubitum quartae, pectus item quartae. Reliqua quoque membra suas habent commensus proportiones, quibus etiam antiqui pictores et statuarii nobiles usi magnas et infinitas laudes sunt adsecuti.

Traducción

For nature has so composed the human body that the face, from the chin to the top of the forehead at the roots of the hair, is a tenth part of the stature; the outstretched hand, from the wrist to the tip of the middle finger, as much again. The head, from the chin to the crown, is an eighth part; from the top of the breast, including the neck, to the roots of the hair, a sixth; and from the middle of the breast to the crown, a fourth. Of the height of the face itself, a third runs from the base of the chin to the underside of the nose; the nose, from its base to the line of the brows, as much again; and thence to the roots of the hair the forehead is formed, likewise a third. The foot is a sixth part of the body's height; the forearm a fourth; and the breast likewise a fourth. The other members too have their proportions of commensurability, of which the ancient painters and the celebrated sculptors availed themselves to win great and boundless praise.

Traducción

Vitr., De arch. III.1.5

Latín

Nec minus mensurarum rationes, quae in omnibus operibus videntur necessariae esse, ex corporis membris collegerunt, uti digitum, palmum, pedem, cubitum, et eas distribuerunt in perfectum numerum, quem Graeci τέλειον dicunt. Perfectum autem antiqui instituerunt numerum qui decem dicitur; namque ex manibus digitorum numero [ab palmo pes] est inventus. Si autem in utrisque palmis ex articulis ab natura decem sunt perfecti, etiam Platoni placuit esse eum numerum ea re perfectum, quod ex ⟨X⟩ singularibus rebus, quae μονάδες apud Graecos dicuntur, perficitur decusis. Simul autem undecim aut duodecim sunt facti, quod superaverint, non possunt esse perfecti, donec ad alterum decusis perveniant; singulares enim res particulae sunt eius numeri.

Traducción

They also derived from the members of the body the units of measure required in every work—the finger, palm, foot and cubit—and distributed them according to the perfect number, which the Greeks call teleion (τέλειον). The ancients established ten as the perfect number, for it was discovered from the number of fingers on the hands. Since the fingers articulated in both hands amount by nature to ten, Plato too considered ten perfect because the decad is completed from ten single units, called monades (μονάδες) by the Greeks. Once eleven or twelve is reached, however, the excess cannot be perfect until the second decad is attained; single units are particles of that number.

Vitr., De arch. III.1.6

Latín

Mathematici vero contra disputantes ea re perfectum dixerunt esse numerum qui sex dicitur, quod is numerus habet partitiones eorum rationibus sex numero convenientes sic: sextantem unum, trientes duo, semissem tria, besem quem δίμοιρον dicunt quattuor, quintarium quem πεντάμοιρον dicunt quinque, perfectum sex. Cum ad duplicationem crescat, supra sex adiecto asse ἔφεκτον; cum facta sunt octo, quod est tertia adiecta, tertiarium alterum, qui ἐπίτριτος dicitur; dimidia adiecta cum facta sunt novem, sesquialterum, qui ἡμιόλιος appellatur; duabus partibus additis et decusis facto bes alterum, quem ἐπιδίμοιρον vocitant; in undecim numero quod adiecti sunt quinque, quintarium, quem ἐπίπεμπτον dicunt; duodecim autem, quod ex duobus numeris simplicibus est effectus, διπλάσιονα.

Traducción

The mathematicians, arguing the contrary, called six the perfect number because its constituent divisions correspond to it: a sixth is one, a third two and a half three; two-thirds, called dimoiron (δίμοιρον), are four; five-sixths, called pentamoiron (πεντάμοιρον), are five; and the complete number is six. In the progression beyond six, adding one produces seven, the ephekton (ἔφεκτον); adding a third produces eight, the epitritos (ἐπίτριτος); adding a half produces nine, the sesquialter or hemiolios (ἡμιόλιος); adding two-thirds produces ten, the epidimoiron (ἐπιδίμοιρον); adding five produces eleven, the epipempton (ἐπίπεμπτον); and twelve, made from two complete sixes, is diplasion (διπλάσιον), the double.

Vitr., De arch. III.1.7

Latín

Non minus etiam, quod pes hominis altitudinis sextam habet partem, (ita etiam, ex eo quod perficitur pedum numero corporis ⟨altitudo, veteribus columnis basis scapi elata⟩ sexies altitudines terminavit) eum perfectum constituerunt, cubitumque animadverterunt ex sex palmis constare digitisque XXIIII. Traducción

They also regarded six as perfect because the human foot is a sixth of the body’s height (the body’s height is completed by six foot-lengths; likewise, in ancient columns, the height of the shaft with its base was determined as sixfold). They observed that the cubit comprises six palms and twenty-four fingers. The Greek cities appear to have followed the same pattern: just as the cubit contains six palms, they established six equally valued bronze pieces, like asses, in the drachma which they used as currency, calling them obols; quarter-obols, called dichalca by some and trichalca by others, numbered twenty-four to the drachma, corresponding to the fingers.

Vitr., De arch. III.1.8

Latín

Nostri autem primo fecerunt ⟨perfectum⟩ antiquum numerum et in denario denos aeris constituerunt, et ea re compositio nominis ad hodiernum diem denarium retinet. Etiamque quarta pars quod efficiebatur ex duobus assibus et tertio semisse, sestertium vocitaverunt. Postea autem quam animadverterunt utrosque numeros esse perfectos, et sex et decem, utrosque in unum coiecerunt et fecerunt perfectissimum decusis sexis. Huius autem rei auctorem invenerunt pedem. E cubito enim cum dempti sunt palmi duo, relinquitur pes quattuor palmorum, palmus autem habet quattuor digitos. Ita efficitur, ut habeat pes sedecim digitos et totidem asses aeracius denarius.

Traducción

Our own people, on the other hand, first adopted the ancient perfect number and fixed ten bronze asses to the denarius; and hence the composition of the name preserves to this day the 'denarius.' And the quarter part, which was made up of two asses and a semis, they called the sestertius. But afterwards, observing that both numbers were perfect—six and ten—they combined them into one and formed the most perfect number, sixteen. Of this they found the foot to be the author: for, when two palms are taken from the cubit, there remains the foot of four palms, and the palm has four fingers; so it comes about that the foot has sixteen fingers, and the denarius as many bronze asses.

Vitr., De arch. III.2.1

Latín

Aedium autem principia sunt e quibus constat figurarum aspectus. ea primum in antis, quod graece ναὸς ἐν παραστάσιν dicitur, deinde prostylos, amphiprostylos, peripteros, pseudodipteros, <dipteros,> hypaethros. horum exprimuntur formationes his rationibus.

Traducción

Archaeological note — III.2, temple types. Vitruvius classifies temples by the arrangement of their columns: in antis (naos en parastasin), prostyle, amphiprostyle, peripteral, pseudodipteral, dipteral and hypaethral. Archaeology illustrates each type: Vitruvius himself exemplifies the in antis temple, with two columns between the antae, by the “Three Fortunes” near Rome’s Colline Gate; the peripteral scheme, with six columns at the front and eleven on each side, is that of the canonical Greek temple; the pseudodipteral arrangement, octastyle with a single row where a double row could stand, is exemplified by the temple of Diana at Magnesia, by Hermogenes of Alabanda, and that of Apollo, by Menesthes; the dipteral, with its double colonnade, and the hypaethral, decastyle and open to the sky, complete the sequence. The pseudoperipteral type—columns engaged in the cella wall to simulate a peripteral arrangement, defined by Vitruvius in 4.8—was the most widespread in the Roman world. Its best-preserved examples are the temple of Portunus in the Forum Boarium, Ionic and dating to the late second century BC, and above all the Maison Carrée at Nîmes, a Corinthian hexastyle prostyle temple of around AD 2, the world’s best-preserved Roman temple.

Latín

Traducción

A peripteral temple has six columns at the front and rear, and eleven on each side including the corner columns. The examples, without a rear part in the adopted wording, are the temple of Jupiter Stator by Hermodorus in the Portico of Metellus and Marius’s temple of Honor and Virtue, built by Mucius.

There is no example at Rome; at Athens the text gives the octastyle temple of Olympian Zeus.

Vitr., De arch. III.3.2

Latín

ergo pycnostylos est cuius intercolumnio unius et dimidiatae columnae crassitudo interponi potest, quemadmodum est divi Iulii et in Caesaris foro Veneris et si quae aliae sic sunt compositae. item systylos est in qua duarum columnarum crassitudo in intercolumnio poterit conlocari, et spirarum plinthides aeque magnae sint ei spatio quod fuerit inter duas plinthides, quemadmodum est Fortunae Equestris ad theatrum lapideum reliquaeque quae eisdem rationibus sunt compositae.

Latín

quo altius enim scandit oculi species, non facile persecat aeris crebritatem. dilapsa itaque altitudinis spatio et viribus exuta incertam modulorum renuntiat sensibus quantitatem. quare semper adiciendum est rationis supplementum in symmetriarum membris, ut cum fuerint aut altioribus locis opera aut etiam ipsa colossicotera, habeant magnitudinum rationem. epistylii latitudo in imo quod supra capitulum erit quanta crassitudo summae columnae sub capitulo erit tanta fiat, summum quantum imus scapus.

Traducción

The higher the line of sight rises, the less readily it penetrates the density of the air. Weakened and dispersed over the distance, it reports an uncertain perception of the modules to the senses. A proportional allowance must therefore be added to the members, so that works placed higher up, or themselves of colossal scale, retain an appropriate apparent magnitude. The architrave’s width at the bottom, where it rests on the capital, is to equal the upper diameter of the column beneath the capital; at the top it is to equal the lower diameter of the shaft.

Latín

cymatium epistylii septima parte suae altitudinis est faciendum, et in proiectura tantundem. reliqua pars praeter cymatium dividenda est in partes XII et earum trium ima fascia est facienda, secunda IIII, summa V. item zophorus supra epistylium quarta parte minus ⟨altus⟩ quam epistylium, sin autem sigilla designari oportuerit, quarta parte altior quam epistylium, uti auctoritatem habeant scalpturae. cymatium suae altitudinis partis septimae, proiecturae cymatiûm quantum crassitudo.

Traducción

The cyma of the architrave is to be made one-seventh of its height, and its projection the same. The rest, apart from the cyma, is to be divided into twelve parts: of these the lowest fascia is made of three, the second of four, and the top of five. Likewise the frieze above the architrave is one-quarter lower than the architrave; but if figures are to be carved upon it, a fourth part taller than the architrave, so that the carvings may have prominence. Its cyma is a seventh part of its own height, and the projection of the cyma as much as its thickness.

Latín

supra zophorum denticulus est faciendus tam altus quam epistylii media fascia, proiectura eius quantum altitudo. intersectio, quae graece μετόπη dicitur, sic est dividenda uti denticulus altitudinis suae dimidiam partem habeat in fronte, cavus autem intersectionis huius frontis e tribus duas partes, huius cymatium altitudinis eius sextam partem. corona cum suo cymatio, praeter simam, quantum media fascia epistylii, proiectura coronae cum denticulo facienda est quantum erit altitudo a zophoro ad summum coronae cymatium, et omnino omnes ecphorae venustiorem habeant speciem quae quantum altitudinis tantundem habent proiecturae.

Traducción

Above the frieze, make the dentil course as high as the middle fascia of the architrave, with its projection equal to its height. Divide the spacing, called metope (μετόπη) in Greek, so that each dentil’s front width is half its height and the gap between dentils is two-thirds of that front width. Its cymatium is one-sixth of its height. The cornice, including its cymatium but excluding the sima, is to be as high as the architrave’s middle fascia. The combined projection of cornice and dentils is to equal the height from the frieze to the top of the cornice’s cymatium. In general, projecting members have a more graceful appearance when their projection equals their height.

Latín

tympani autem quod est in fastigio altitudo sic est facienda uti frons coronae ab extremis cymatiis tota dimetiatur in partes novem et ex eis una pars in medio cacumini tympani constituatur, dum contra epistylia columnarumque hypotrachelia ad perpendiculum respondeant. coronaeque supra aequaliter imis praeter simas sunt conlocandae. insuper coronas simae, quas Graeci ἐπαιετίδες dicunt, faciendae sunt altiores octava parte coronarum altitudinis. acroteria angularia tam alta quantum est tympanum medium, mediana altiora octava parte quam angularia.

Traducción

To determine the height of the tympanum within the pediment, divide the entire front of the cornice, measured between the outer ends of its cymatia, into nine parts. Give one part to the height at the tympanum’s central apex, while keeping its face vertically aligned with the architraves and the necks of the columns. The upper cornices are to match the lower ones, apart from the simae. Above the cornices, the simae, called epaetides in Greek, are to be one-eighth taller than the cornices. The corner acroteria are to be as high as the tympanum at its center; the central acroteria are to be one-eighth taller than the corner ones.

Latín

Membra omnia quae supra capitula columnarum sunt futura id est epistylia zophora coronae tympana fastigia acroteria inclinanda sunt in fronte suae cuiusque altitudinis parte XII, ideo quod cum steterimus contra frontes, ab oculo lineae duae si extensae fuerint et una tetigerit imam operis partem, altera summam, quae summam tetigerit longior fiet. ita quo longior visus linea in superiorem partem procedit, resupinatam facit eius speciem. cum autem, uti supra scriptum est, in fronte inclinata fuerint, tunc in aspectu videbuntur esse ad perpendiculum et normam.

Traducción

All members above the column capitals—architraves, friezes, cornices, tympana, pediments and acroteria—must incline forwards by one-twelfth of their respective heights. When we stand facing the facade, two lines drawn from the eye to the bottom and the top of the work have different lengths: the line reaching the top is longer. As the line of sight extends farther upwards, it makes the upper part appear to lean backwards. If the members incline forwards as prescribed, they will appear plumb and square to the viewer.

Latín

in simis quae supra coronam in lateribus sunt aedium, capita leonina sunt scalpenda, disposita ita uti contra columnas singulas primum sint designata, cetera aequali modo disposita uti singula singulis mediis tegulis respondeant. haec autem quae erunt contra columnas perterebrata sint ad canalem qui excipit e tegulis aquam caelestem, mediana autem sint solida, uti quae cadit vis aquae per tegulas in canalem ne deiciatur per intercolumnia neque transeuntes perfundat, sed quae sunt contra columnas videantur emittere vomentia ructus aquarum ex ore. Traducción

Carve lions’ heads in the simae above the cornice along the sides of the temples. Set one opposite each column, then arrange the rest evenly, each corresponding to the center of a roof tile. Pierce those opposite the columns through to the channel that receives rainwater from the tiles; leave the intermediate ones solid, so that the water falling from the tiles into the channel does not pour down through the intercolumniations or drench passers-by. The heads opposite the columns will appear to vomit streams of water from their mouths. In this volume I have described the arrangements of Ionic temples as clearly as I could; I shall explain the proportions of Doric and Corinthian temples in the following book.
