Latín

Maiores cum sapienter tum etiam utiliter instituerunt, per commentariorum relationes cogitata tradere posteris, ut ea non interirent sed singulis aetatibus crescentia voluminibus edita gradatim pervenirent vetustatibus ad summam doctrinarum subtilitatem. itaque non mediocres sed infinitae sunt his agendae gratiae, quod non invidiose silentes praetermiserunt, sed omnium generum sensus conscriptionibus memoriae tradendos curaverunt.

Traducción

The ancients, both wisely and usefully, instituted the transmission of their thoughts to posterity through the reports of their commentaries, so that they would not perish, but, published in increasing volumes in each age, would gradually reach the highest subtlety of their doctrines. Therefore, the merits of this work are not moderate but infinite, because they did not pass over it in envious silence, but took care to transmit to memory all kinds of thoughts in writings.

Traducción

Those who claim glory by violating the work of others, instead of relying on their own ideas, merit not merely reproach but punishment for acting dishonourably. Such offenses, it is reported, did not go unpunished among the ancients. It is therefore appropriate to recount the outcomes of those judgments as they have been handed down to us.

Traducción

And to sum up, his death as a condemned parricide is variously recorded. For some have written that he was crucified at Philadelphia, some that he was stoned at Chios, others that he was thrown alive on a pyre at Smyrna. Whether any of these things happened to him, the punishment he deserved was decidedly worthy. For it does not seem that he deserves otherwise who quotes those whose response, what they thought when writing, cannot be stated in person.

Archaeological note — Bk. 7.1 (pavements). Vitruvius transmits the ancient recipe for the layered Roman floor, which archaeology confirms across the Empire (from Fishbourne in Britain to Lod in Israel, from Zippori to Braga). From the bottom up: the statumen (large stones forming the base and draining ground moisture); the rudus or ruderatio (a mortar of broken brick and lime, more than 20 cm thick, rammed down —three parts lime to one if fresh, five if recycled—); the nucleus (a fine layer of three parts crushed potsherd to one of lime, some 15 cm); and above it the setting bed where the tesserae of the pavimentum were fixed. On upper floors the whole is laid over the joists. A key material here is opus signinum (cocciopesto): lime with crushed terracotta, waterproof, of Punic North African origin, spread through Sicily and Italy and used both for floors and for cisterns. The Vitruvian stratigraphy remains the reference framework for the archaeometry of mosaics.
